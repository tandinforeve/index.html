Kinematics

Kinematics describes motion without asking what causes it. With constant acceleration: v = u + at, s = ut + ½at², v² = u² + 2as. A projectile moves at constant speed horizontally and accelerates at g vertically.

Projectile launcher
My comment: I used s = ut + ½at² the most, because the height and the range of the launcher both come from it once you split the speed into horizontal and vertical parts. What surprised me about the 45° result is that it only works when the ball lands at the same height it was launched from. When I tried 30° and 60°, the ranges came out the same, because sin 2θ has the same value for angles that add up to 90°. Basketball players know this without the equation: a shot from the free-throw line needs a high arc, and a flatter, faster shot works better when the player is close to the hoop.

Frame of reference

Motion is always measured relative to something. A ball thrown straight up inside a moving train goes straight up and down for a passenger, but traces a parabola for someone on the ground. Both observers are right.

Ball thrown up in a train
My comment: When I travel on the bus on the winding road between Trashigang and Thimphu, the trees and houses seem to rush backwards, while the person sitting next to me looks perfectly still. When another bus overtakes us slowly, it looks as if it is barely moving, but to someone standing on the road both buses are moving fast. This showed me that speed has no meaning until we say what we are measuring it against.

Friction and its laws

Limiting friction is the maximum static friction, F = μN. The angle of limiting friction λ satisfies tan λ = μ. The angle of repose is the steepest slope on which a body stays at rest, and it equals λ. Air resistance grows with speed until it balances weight, giving a terminal velocity.

Block on an incline
Falling with air resistance
My comment: When I walk, my foot pushes backwards on the ground and static friction pushes me forwards. Without it, like on wet stones or ice in winter, my foot just slides and I cannot move forward. This is why shoes have grooves and why basketball shoes grip the court so well. A parachute has a large area, so the air resistance becomes large at a low speed. The terminal velocity √(mg/k) falls when k increases, and in my simulation, increasing the drag made the speed settle at a much lower value, which is a safe speed to land.

Work-energy theorem

The net work done on a body equals its change in kinetic energy.

Wnet = ΔKE = ½mv² − ½mu²
Pushing a block from rest
My comment: With F = ma I would need to find the acceleration, then the time or use another equation, before I get the final speed. With the work-energy theorem, I only need the total work done and the starting and finishing speeds, and I do not need to know the time or how the force changed along the way. It is also useful when the path is curved or the force is not constant, such as a ball sliding down a curved slide, because only the work done matters and not the shape of the path.

Newton's law in terms of momentum

Momentum is p = mv. Newton's second law in its general form says the net force is the rate of change of momentum.

F = dp/dt
Constant force on a cart
My comment: F = ma only works when the mass stays the same. A rocket burns fuel and throws it out of the back, so its mass keeps decreasing. Writing the law as F = dp/dt takes care of this, because we look at the change in momentum of the whole system, and the momentum gained by the rocket is equal to the momentum carried away by the exhaust gas. That is why a rocket can speed up in empty space where there is nothing to push against. The same idea applies to a raindrop that collects more water as it falls.

Law of conservation of momentum

If no external force acts on a system, its total momentum stays constant. Elastic collisions (e = 1) also conserve kinetic energy. Perfectly inelastic collisions (e = 0) make the bodies stick together.

Two-cart collision
My comment: The kinetic energy is not destroyed, it is changed into other forms. When the carts stick together, some energy becomes heat, sound and the work done in deforming the carts. Momentum is still conserved, since no external force acts on the system, but kinetic energy is not. As a chemistry student, I find this interesting because it is similar to colliding molecules. In a gas the collisions are almost elastic, so the molecules keep their energy, but in a reaction some collision energy can go into breaking and forming bonds.

Impulse and applications

Impulse is force times the time it acts, and it equals the change in momentum. It is the area under a force-time graph.

J = FavgΔt = Δp = m(v − u)
Ball hitting a wall

Applications: airbags and crumple zones lengthen the stopping time, which lowers the force. A cricketer pulls the hands back while catching, and bending your knees when landing does the same job. A golf club or bat does the opposite: a short, hard contact gives a large force.

My comment: The one that means the most to me is seat belts and airbags, because it connects to the roads in Bhutan, which have many sharp bends and steep drops. In a crash the change in momentum of the passenger is fixed, but a seat belt that stretches a little and an airbag that squashes make the stopping time longer, so the average force on the body becomes smaller (F = Δp/Δt). I also see it when I play basketball. When I catch a fast pass, I bring my hands back with the ball, and when I land from a jump I bend my knees. Both make the stopping time longer and protect my fingers and knees.

Unit reflection

What I learned. In this unit I moved from simply describing motion in kinematics to explaining it with forces, energy and momentum. I now see these ideas as connected: the work-energy theorem and the impulse-momentum theorem are both consequences of Newton's laws. Learning them helped me understand how forces work in a much better way than before

What was challenging. The hardest part was the signs of the velocities in collisions. At first I got wrong answers because I forgot that a cart moving to the left has a negative velocity, and the momentum before and after did not match. The simulation helped, because I could set v₂ to a negative value and see the answer change. I also found it difficult to decide between the work-energy method and the equations of motion, and I learned that it is easier when the question has no time in it.

What the simulations taught me. Changing one variable at a time showed me things equations alone did not, such as how the angle of repose depends only on μ, and how a longer contact time reduces force without changing impulse.

Real-life connections and next steps. Mechanics now appears everywhere I look. In basketball, the angle and speed of a shot are projectile motion, and following through on a pass or a catch is impulse. On the road, seat belts, crumple zones and the stopping distance of a vehicle on a wet road depend on momentum, energy and friction. In engineering, bridges and buildings must handle forces and friction, and in chemistry, collision ideas explain gas pressure and reaction rates. Next I want to study circular motion and gravitation, then rotational motion, because I think they build on the same ideas. To improve, I will practise more numerical problems, draw a clear diagram with the directions of forces and velocities before I calculate, and check my answers using both momentum and energy where possible.
